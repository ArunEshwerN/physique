Lean, not large.

A twelve-month plan for a specific build — wide shoulders, a full upper chest, lat width, visible abs — using one adjustable bench, two dumbbells, a pull-up bar and sixty-four kilograms of cast iron. Four sessions a week, none of them longer than an hour.

Block Zero comes first

Everything on this page waits until the first clean unassisted pull-up. That block — the band ladder, the session protocol, the posture routine, and a tracker that moves the projected date with your real pace — lives on its own page: The Pull-Up Block →

One thing carries back here: hold your weight flat until that block is done. Your bodyweight is the load on a pull-up, so the surplus below starts the week you get your first unassisted rep — not before.

Before anything else

The honest read

At 64.5 kg having never lifted, getting bulky is not a risk you need to manage. It is the opposite problem. The physiques you're aiming at sit around 10–13% body fat with real shoulder, upper-chest and lat mass. Daniel Craig in Casino Royale was roughly 78 kg at 178 cm.

Your 70 kg ceiling is sensible and achievable — and you will not look blocky there. You'll look like the reference photos. But understand the direction of travel: hitting that look requires getting to roughly that number. The cap stays. The gaining is not optional.

Working assumption

Height taken as 168–175 cm. Under 165 cm, shift the target band to 63–66 kg. Over 178 cm, allow 72 kg. Nothing else in the plan changes.

What the look actually requires

Strip away the lighting and the tans and every one of those photos has the same four things: side delts, a full upper chest, lat width, and low enough body fat to see the abs. Arms matter. Legs are essentially invisible. So the program is shoulder- and upper-body-biased, with legs trained enough for health, proportion and injury resistance — but never chased.

Equipment

Two bars, twenty-four plates, one ceiling

The bars

Buy two of the 45 cm threaded bar (ID 8766096), ₹999 each. Not the 35 cm. The reason is safety, not capacity.

The 35 cm bar relies on Smart Disc Collars, rated 15 kg in a vertical position. Every overhead press, every incline press, every lateral raise is a vertical position — and you'll pass 15 kg per dumbbell inside four months. From then on you'd be pressing weight over your face held on by a collar past its rating. The threaded bar's locknuts thread onto the bar itself: 30 kg per side, no such limit. ₹800 more for the pair.

Don't

Skip 50 mm Olympic. The handles are thick, the empty bar weighs 5–7 kg, and the plates are enormous. For dumbbell-only training at 70 kg bodyweight it is not a limitation you will ever meet. 28 mm standard with locknuts is correct. On "19 inch" — 45 cm is 17.7", already the upper end of comfortable. Longer bars clip your ribcage on bench press and your ears overhead.

The plates

Cast iron only. Never 10 kg or 20 kg plates for dumbbells. The 10 kg disc is 295 mm across and the 20 kg is 360 mm — on a dumbbell that means the plate hangs to your knee on a row and hits the bench on a press. Cast iron 5 kg is 230 mm against rubber's 252 mm: denser, more compact, exactly what a short bar wants.

The answer to your question

34.3 kg per dumbbell is your ceiling and you will never need more. A 70 kg natural lifter pressing a pair of 34 kg dumbbells for eight reps is a genuinely strong intermediate-to-advanced lifter — a three-to-five-year destination. Lifetime spend including bars: ₹14,774.

On the 1.25 kg plates

Skip them. Not because the plate is bad — because it doesn't buy you a finer step. A pair of 1.25 kg discs moves a dumbbell by +2.5 kg, which is coarser than the +2 kg the 1 kg plates already give you, and it lands your loads on half-kilos that don't line up with anything else in the chart. Two plate sizes doing almost the same job is how a home gym ends up cluttered.

If you ever want a genuinely smaller jump, the plate to look for is the 0.5 kg cast iron (part of range ref 1042303). Four of those give you +1 kg per dumbbell — a real improvement, and only worth it for lateral raises, where +2 kg on a 6 kg dumbbell is a 33% jump. Low priority. Buy them in year two if the laterals start stalling, not now.

Phase 0 · Weeks 1–4

Foundation

You've never lifted. The single biggest cause of quitting in month one is destroying yourself in week one and being unable to walk. So the first four weeks are deliberately, almost frustratingly easy.

Read this twice

Do not add extra sets. Do not go to failure. You should leave every session feeling like you could have done twice as much. That feeling is the point. Tendons adapt roughly three times slower than muscle — this month is for them.

Chin-up progression — follow in order

  1. Dead hang. Hang from the bar, shoulders pulled down, not shrugged into your ears. 3 × 20 s. Stay here until you can hang 45 s.
  2. Negatives. Step or jump to the top, chin over bar, then lower as slowly as you can. Target 5 s down. 2 × 5.
  3. Band-assisted chin-ups. Loop the band over the bar, foot in it. 2 × 5.
  4. Bodyweight chin-ups.

Most people get their first one somewhere in months two to four. It comes.

The engine

Progression

This is the part that makes the plan work. Every exercise has a rep range and a set count. You climb the reps, then you add weight and start again. That's the whole method.

Double progression, worked through

Week 212 kg9 · 9 · 8
Week 312 kg10 · 10 · 9
Week 412 kg11 · 10 · 10
Week 512 kg12 · 12 · 11
Week 612 kg12 · 12 · 12 ← top of range

The six rules

  1. Pick your starting weight so you can hit the bottom of the range with about 3 reps left in reserve. If the range is 8–12, pick a weight you could do 11 times — and stop at 8.
  2. Every session, try to add one rep to each set. Same weight.
  3. Only add weight when all sets hit the top of the range with clean form. Not two out of three. All of them.
  4. The jump is +2 kg per dumbbell — one 1 kg plate per side. That's your smallest increment and it's the right one.
  5. A rep drop after a jump is normal. Twelve reps becoming seven is expected. Grind back up.
  6. If you fail to add a rep on any set two sessions running, drop 4 kg per dumbbell and rebuild. This happens maybe twice a year. It's the system working, not failing.

Safety · no rack, no spotter

Never take DB bench press or overhead press to true failure. If you can't complete a rep on bench, don't fight it — let the dumbbells drop out to the sides as you sit up. Practise that once with a light weight so the movement is automatic when you need it.

The long view

Blocks and deloads

Every eight weeks, take a deload week. Same exercises, half the sets, 60% of the weight. You will feel like you're wasting a week. You are not — this is where tendons catch up and accumulated fatigue clears. The people who skip deloads are the people who get hurt in month seven.

No calorie counting

Nutrition, in four rules

You don't need to track. You need four rules and a bathroom scale.

Rule 1 — Protein: about 120 g a day

Roughly 1.8 g per kg. Don't weigh it — count portions. One palm-sized serving of a protein food is about 25–30 g. You need four of them a day, spread out.

Palm-sized servings: 150 g chicken · 2 eggs plus 100 g paneer · 150 g fish · 200 g Greek yoghurt · one scoop of whey · 250 g dal with rice (weaker — count it as 0.7).

If you're vegetarian this is the hardest part of the whole plan, and whey protein is worth the money. Two scoops a day covers half your target.

Rule 2 — Gain slowly: 0.5–0.75 kg a month

That's roughly +250 kcal above maintenance — for you, one extra meaningful snack a day. A handful of nuts and a banana. A glass of full-fat milk with peanut butter. That's it.

Gaining faster than 1 kg a month at your training age adds fat, not muscle — and fat is precisely what stands between you and those photos. At this rate: 64.5 kg → 70 kg in roughly 9–11 months, and as a total beginner training four days a week, the great majority of it will be muscle.

Rule 3 — The weigh-in protocol

Weigh yourself every morning: after the bathroom, before eating, same conditions. Daily weight swings 1–2 kg from water and food, so ignore individual days entirely. Every Sunday, average the week's seven numbers. That average is your only real data point.

Rule 4 — When to stop gaining

Two triggers, whichever comes first:

  • You hit 70 kg, or
  • Your abs stop being visible in normal light — not flexed, not in a mirror at a good angle, just standing there

When either happens, stop adding calories and hold your weight flat for 4–8 weeks while you keep training. You'll keep getting stronger and slowly leaner.

If you've added noticeable fat, run a mini-cut: drop about 400 kcal a day for 6–8 weeks, keep protein at 120 g, and keep lifting the exact same program at the exact same weights. Lose 0.4–0.5 kg a week, no faster. Then return to maintenance.

Slow gain → hold → short cut → slow gain. That cycle is how you live in the 12–15% range permanently instead of yo-yoing.

Cardio, steps and sleep

  • 8,000–10,000 steps a day. The most underrated tool for staying lean without dieting hard, and it costs you no recovery.
  • Two easy 30-minute walks, Wednesday and Saturday, as scheduled.
  • No running, no HIIT. They eat recovery and buy you little at your bodyweight. If you enjoy them, cap at one session a week and put it on Saturday.
  • Sleep 7.5–8 hours. Non-negotiable. Under-sleeping kills recovery, kills training performance, and drives appetite for exactly the wrong foods. Given the choice between an extra workout and an extra hour of sleep — sleep.

What to expect

Timeline

  • Month 1Movements start feeling natural. Little visible change. This is normal and it is not a sign anything is wrong.
  • Month 3Around 66 kg. First unassisted chin-ups. Shoulders visibly rounder. Clothes fit differently.
  • Month 6Around 67–68 kg. The upper-chest shelf appears. Lat spread visible from behind. Bench around 20 kg per hand.
  • Month 9–11Around 70 kg. This is where people start commenting.
  • Month 12–14 · the targetHold weight, or a short mini-cut, landing at 68–70 kg and roughly 12–13%. This is the photo.
  • Year 2–3Same bodyweight, better everywhere. Bench 26–30 kg per hand. A genuinely impressive physique.
What actually decides this

Two things determine whether you get there, and neither is exercise selection: did you train four times a week for a hundred weeks, and did you log every session so the progression was real rather than imagined. Everything above is built so that both stay easy. Nothing in it should ever leave you too wrecked to show up on Thursday.
