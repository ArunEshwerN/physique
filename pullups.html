Non-negotiable

The rules

  1. The pain screen runs first, every time. Three easy scapular reps. Anything sharp ends the session — no debate, no negotiating with yourself at the bar.
  2. Every rep stage gets cleared twice. Two clean sessions to advance a stage, all stages to clear a rung. Your rule, and the right one.
  3. A new rung restarts at 3/3/3. Dropping band assistance raises the load, so the volume drops to meet it. You climb one rep at a time from there to 5/5/5.
  4. One cue only: clap the armpits. Never swap cues mid-set, never stack two. Boring is the point.
  5. A scraped rep voids the session. Not a punishment — information. You logged it, you repeat it, you move on.
  6. Three sessions a week, Monday / Wednesday / Friday. Miss one, shift forward. Never two pull-up sessions on consecutive days.
  7. If a rung takes more than fourteen sessions past its minimum, go back up one mark and re-clear it. You lose two weeks and gain the rep quality that was missing.
On the eleven-day break

You noticed the cue took a few sets to come back while the strength was still there. Worth knowing permanently: after a layoff the skill returns before the strength was ever lost. A week or two off costs a session or two of re-learning, not weeks of progress. Never panic-restart — re-enter at the same rung and the same reps.

The habit change — this matters more than the routine

Take the Tall Standing Reset out of the routine and into your day. Three times daily, twenty seconds, whenever you remember — waiting for a kettle, in a lift, before you sit down at your desk. Keep it in the routine too.

Posture is not a strength problem, it's a default position problem. Ten minutes on a mat teaches the shape; standing in it sixty times a week makes it the one you fall into without thinking. The mat work alone will plateau. This is the part that transfers.

Short version, when time is tight

  • Pelvic tilts — 10
  • Glute bridges — 2 × 10
  • Hip flexor stretch — 30 s per side
  • Tall standing reset — 2 × 20 s

Four minutes, and genuinely enough on a bad day.

Do you keep it once the main program starts?

Yes, but it shrinks. Phase 1 already does most of this under load — Romanian deadlifts and hip thrusts train the exact hip extension the tilt is missing, hanging knee raises train anti-extension harder than a dead bug ever will, split squats stretch one hip flexor while the other works. So after unassisted:

  • Full routine — twice a week, on rest days
  • Hip flexor stretch + tall standing reset — daily, two minutes
  • Tall standing reset in the wild — 3× a day, forever
On the week you missed

Nothing has been undone. Posture work is motor learning, not fitness — you're teaching a default position, and skills decay far slower than conditioning. A week off through illness costs a couple of rusty sessions. Pick it up where you left it, and don't add extra sessions to compensate — that's the impulse that turns a missed week into a quit month.

On the tilt

I can't diagnose from photographs and won't pretend otherwise. But I can tell you what they're consistent with, and more usefully, what your own second photo already proved.

What the pictures show

Standing relaxed, there's a mild forward tilt of the pelvis with the lower abdomen carried forward and a lumbar curve slightly deeper than neutral. That is mild anterior pelvic tilt, and it is unremarkable — a large majority of desk-working adults measure some. It is a position, not an injury.

A good part of what reads as tilt isn't tilt at all — it's a relaxed abdominal wall over a normal amount of subcutaneous fat, photographed standing at ease. Those look nearly identical in a mirror and are completely different problems. The first is a habit. The second is answered by the nutrition section of the main plan over months, not by any exercise.

Your second photo is the whole answer

You pushed the hips forward and the stomach tucked in by itself. That means you can voluntarily control your pelvic position — so this is postural and motor-control driven, not structural. Structural tilt doesn't move when you ask it to. Yours moved. That's genuinely good news, and it means the routine you're already doing is aimed at exactly the right target.

A self-test worth doing once

  1. Stand with your back flat against a wall, heels about 5 cm out, shoulders and head touching.
  2. Stand normally. Don't correct anything.
  3. Slide a flat hand into the gap at your lower back.
  • Palm slides in snugly, not much more — normal. Nothing to fix.
  • Palm plus a lot of room, or your whole fist — meaningful tilt. Worth the ten minutes a day you're already spending.
  • No gap at all — the opposite pattern, also fine.

Do it once. Again in three months. Not weekly — you'll just make yourself anxious about noise.

What actually changes it

  1. How you stand and sit for the other fifteen hours. This is 80% of it, and it's why the standing reset moved into your day.
  2. Hip flexor length and glute strength. Covered — bridges now, hip thrusts and RDLs later.
  3. Anti-extension core control. Dead bugs now, hanging knee raises later.
  4. Losing abdominal fat. Slow, and mostly a nutrition question.

You're already doing the first three. Don't go looking for more posture exercises — the internet will happily sell you a forty-minute daily protocol for a problem you're already solving with ten.

And you noticed something real: pull-ups do make you stand taller. Hanging decompresses the spine, and the lats and mid-back you're building are the muscles that hold a tall chest without you having to think about it. The pull-up block is posture work.

Easy to get wrong

Weight, while you climb

The main plan says eat +250 kcal and gain to 70 kg. Do not start that yet. A pull-up is the one lift where your bodyweight is the load. Every kilogram makes every remaining rung measurably harder — you'd be climbing a ladder while someone raises it.
